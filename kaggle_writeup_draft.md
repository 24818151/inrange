### 1. Introduction: A Tale of Two Physics Domains
I received the email from my research supervisor--Prof. de Villiers--about the Inrange student competition and was at first going to ignore it, as with most of these types of emails, but also because I am quite busy with my thesis write-up and master's finalisation.
However, I am familiar with Inrange and my curiosity was piqued, so I clicked the URL. 

I saw the problem statement and immediately recognised the unique opportunity I have: I can apply the methodology I have been developing for the past several months for my Master's research at Stellenbosch University: **Physics-Informed Surrogate Modeling**.
By leveraging my strong position and proficiency with agentic LLM usage, I knew I could complete a competitive entry *without committing more than one workday to the project*.

My core research focuses on computational electromagnetics--specifically, building machine learning pipelines to predict the cutoff frequency and impedance of Quad-Ridge Flared Horn (QRFH) antennas. In that domain, I couple deterministic physics solvers (like Mode-Matching algorithms and CST Microwave Studio) with Gaussian Process Regression (GPR). The physics engine provides the structural prior, and the GPR learns the complex, high-dimensional residuals. 

Looking at the Inrange dataset, the similarities are apparent in terms of the problem: Predicting the full trajectory of a golf ball from truncated 60-metre radar data--where 96.3% of the shots haven't even reached their apex--is a severe non-linear extrapolation problem. 

Standard, black-box machine learning models (like XGBoost or Neural Networks) can struggle with this. Give them 491 data points, and they can easily overfit the launch space; ask them to extrapolate 150 metres beyond the training boundary, and they can break down entirely. 

Knowing that Inrange is in large an electromagnetics machine learning company, it followed naturally for me to treat this golf challenge with the same EM-ML philosophy. I didn't just throw AI at a CSV file; I swapped out my CEM solver for an Aerodynamic ODE solver, kept my Bayesian ML architecture intact, and bridged the two domains.

### 2. The Physics Engine: Setting the Prior
Before any machine learning took place, I built a deterministic 3D aerodynamic Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) solver. 
The system models gravity, aerodynamic drag, and the Magnus effect (lift generated by backspin). Using an L-BFGS-B inverse solver, the pipeline fits the optimal drag coefficient (C_d) and lift coefficient (C_l0) for every shot based purely on the 4 spatial checkpoints. 

**Key Physical Insights:**
* **Spin is Vertical, Not Lateral:** Initial intuition suggests lateral drift might act as a proxy for spin. The EDA proved they are uncorrelated (r = -0.08). Instead, the true signal for spin lies in the *vertical lift residual*—how much higher the ball travels compared to a vacuum parabola (Correlation: 0.565).
* **Extracting Session Wind:** The data spans 18 distinct recording sessions. By isolating the lateral drift at the 60m net for the first few shots of a session, the pipeline estimates the ambient wind vector [w_x, w_y]. Feeding this back into the apparent airspeed calculation improves physics prior accuracy.

### 3. The Machine Learning: Cascaded Gaussian Process Regression
While the physics ODE provides an incredibly strong baseline trajectory, it assumes idealised conditions (constant wind, no spin decay, perfect turf). To bridge the gap between idealised physics and reality, the ensemble of Exact Gaussian Processes is deployed.

**The PhysicsFlightMean:** 
Instead of a zero-mean GP, a custom `PhysicsFlightMean` module is utilised. The GP's prior mean is explicitly set to the output of the aerodynamic ODE. The GP is therefore only tasked with learning the residuals (this is one of the key components in my master's research). An Additive Kernel (Linear + Matérn-5/2) is utilised with strict Gamma priors to regularise the hyperparameters, preventing overfitting on the small 491-shot dataset.

**The Cascaded Architecture:**
Predicting a landing coordinate directly from a launch coordinate is quite difficult. To address this, a **Cascaded 2-Stage GPR** is used:
1. **Stage 1** predicts the launch spin rate and the 3D apex coordinates.
2. **Stage 2** predicts the landing coordinates, but it is *conditioned directly on the Stage 1 predicted apex*. 

By mathematically forcing the model to calculate the apex first, I convert a long-range extrapolation problem into a much simpler short-range ballistic descent interpolation. 

### 4. Interactive Physics Dashboard (`app.py`)
To visualise the underlying mechanics, a lightweight Streamlit dashboard was developed that renders any dataset shot in interactive 3D, tracing the launch, radar checkpoints, 60m boundary net, apex, and simulated post-impact bounce & roll. 

It is important to note a deliberate design limitation here: the web app bypasses the heavy PyTorch/GPR machine learning dependencies entirely. It runs *only* the Phase 1 deterministic aerodynamic ODE inverse solver. This ensures the app remains lightweight enough for instant web deployment while isolating and demonstrating the raw strength of the structural physics prior *before* the GPR corrects the final residuals.

### 5. Results & Integrity
The coupling of multi-start ODE optimisation with the cascaded GPR yielded great results during 5-fold cross-validation. While the exact final score is detailed on the leaderboard, the structural integrity of the model is one of its greatest strength.

**Executive Performance Summary (5-Fold CV):**
* **Composite Scaled RMSE:** 0.1630
* **Launch Spin Rate Error:** 540 rpm (MAE)
* **Apex Placement Error:** ~1.36m (Lateral MAE), ~0.56m (Vertical MAE)
* **Landing Placement Error:** ~2.79m (Lateral MAE), ~3.14m (Depth MAE)

### 6. Conclusion
The short project is considered a success as an application of my master's work proving the principles of Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) are domain-agnostic. Robust, extrapolative machine learning doesn't require massive datasets, nor does it require blindly throwing complex architectures at a problem. By treating golf ball aerodynamics with the same rigorous, surrogate-modelling philosophy I use for electromagnetic QRFH antennas--and leveraging agentic LLM workflows to accelerate development--I was able to build the physically constrained, highly accurate pipeline in under a single workday. 

Whether predicting cutoff frequencies for waveguide segments or landing coordinates from truncated radar data, the fundamental solution remains the same: let the deterministic physics provide the structural prior, and let Bayesian inference learn the complex reality.
